Methodology

Data and cohort

Source data are hourly ICU records from the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge 2019, one pipe-separated file per patient. Rows are read in file order as consecutive ICU hours.

Each patient contributes one prediction made at the end of hour 5. Inputs are rows 0–5 only. The outcome is any positive SepsisLabel from row 6 onward. In this dataset the label is already shifted to switch on six hours before the Sepsis-3 onset time, so a positive outcome corresponds to onset at roughly hour 12 or later, and patients with earlier onset are excluded as already labelled in the look-back.

Eligible patients: 1500, of whom 113 have a positive outcome. Excluded: 0 no outcome followup, 0 positive in lookback, 0 too short.

The outcome window runs to the end of each record, so it is longer for patients who stay longer. See the improvement plan for a fixed-horizon alternative.

Features and representations

Tabular models receive 312 engineered features: for each of 39 clinical and demographic variables, the latest observed value, mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, first-to-last change, fraction of hours missing, and an any-missing flag.

The 1D CNN receives the same six hours as an ordered hours × variables tensor without summary statistics or missingness flags. A difference between the CNN and the tabular models therefore reflects both the representation and the model family.

SepsisLabel, the patient identifier and ICULOS are never inputs.

Splitting and preprocessing

Patients are split once into training (1200) and held-out test (300) groups with seed 42 and test fraction 0.2. Every model is scored on the same test patients.

Imputation and scaling are learned from training patients only and then applied unchanged to test patients. No hyperparameters were tuned, and no validation split was used.

Evaluation

Models are ranked by AUPRC, with AUROC as a tie-break. Uncertainty comes from 1000 paired bootstrap resamples of the test patients. A model is called worse than the top model only when the 95% interval of the AUPRC difference excludes zero.

To compare alert burden fairly, each model is also read at the threshold that reaches 80% sensitivity, reporting specificity, PPV and alerts per 100 patients. Calibration is summarised by the logistic calibration slope and a 10-bin calibration curve, and clinical utility by decision-curve net benefit across alert thresholds from 1% to 50%.

Model results

Logistic Regression ranks first on AUPRC (0.236, 3.9× the 6.0% base rate) with AUROC 0.718.

Against XGBoost and Random Forest its AUPRC advantage exceeds resampling noise but AUROC does not separate them, so the gain is in precision among the highest-risk patients rather than in overall ranking.

At 80% sensitivity it raises 62.0 alerts per 100 patients, about 12.4 alerts for every true future case, and misses 1.0 cases per 100 patients.

Only 18 positive patients are in the test set. Intervals are wide and rankings may change with a different split.

Discrimination with uncertainty

Calibration

Points below the diagonal mean the model over-states risk. Class-weighted models are expected to sit below it.

Clinical utility

Net benefit counts true alerts minus false alerts, weighted by how many false alerts are acceptable per true one at each threshold. A model is only useful where its curve sits above both reference lines.
